# Overview
In this module focus, the fundamental concepts of resource management are explored. These concepts include wellbeing (how well a person’s needs are met and how fulfilled they are), needs (essentials for life), and resources (things used to satisfy needs).

# Wellbeing

> [!Note] Students Learn To
> ▪ explore the concept of wellbeing by considering the following questions:
>– what is the opposite to wellbeing? 
> – how do people describe wellbeing?
> – why might there be different understandings of wellbeing?
> 
> ▪ analyse the relationship between the factors and explain how they can impact on wellbeing
> 
> ▪ discuss the effect that their own wellbeing can have on the wellbeing of the groups to which they belong
## Defining Wellbeing

> [!Abstract] Definition
> *Wellbeing is how well a person’s needs are met and how satisfied and fulfilled they are”*. This definition of wellbeing is interpreted differently due to different person factors such as different beliefs, perspectives, and levels of education.
## Factors Affecting Wellbeing
Within the Community and Family Studies course, the concept of wellbeing is deeply explored, with various factors affecting a person’s sense of wellbeing.

These factors of wellbeing include:
- **S**ocial –> Interaction with others, e.g. leisure
- **P**hysical –> Bodily and basic functions, e.g. sleep
- **E**conomic –> Income, money, e.g. job security
- **E**motional –> Feelings, e.g. attachment and bonding
- **C**ultural –> Customs, beliefs, values, e.g. identity and belonging
- **S**piritual –> Morality, life outlook, e.g. ideals, aspirations, religious principles

> [!Tip] Interrelation between the Factors of Wellbeing
> These factors are heavily ==interrelated== to each other. For example, someone going to the the gym five times a week can create negative economic impacts upon a person, but have great physical and social impacts on wellbeing.

The wellbeing of a person is also **ever-changing** and **versatile**.

## Individual and Group Wellbeing
It can also be observed through an individual and group basis.
- The wellbeing of an individual considers their own needs first, with factors including good health, feelings of security, positive relationships, etc.
- Group wellbeing deals with concerns surrounding the needs of the collective (all people), aiming to improve individual wellbeing through resource access for the satisfaction of needs.
- Individual and group wellbeing are not mutually exclusive to another. However, an individual’s wellbeing and the group’s wellbeing can affect each other. For example, if a child’s parents are constantly yelling, the child may feel a negative impact on their emotional wellbeing.

Wellbeing can be measured in the following hierarchy, from lowest to highest.
1. Satisfaction of needs
2. Environmental comfort
3. Effective communication
4. Autonomy
# Needs and Wants

> [!Note] Students Learn To
> ▪ describe each of the specific needs and compare the significance of each to different individuals
> 
> ▪ critique Maslow’s hierarchy and debate its relevance and validity after considering contemporary views on human needs
> 
> ▪ outline a specific need that is significant to them and explain how goal setting can contribute to the satisfaction of that need
## Defining Needs and Wants

> [!Abstract] Definitions
> *Needs are essentials for life, and things that do not change overtime. If needs are not met, they can result in poor health outcomes.* For example, if a person does not have food or water, it can lead to them becoming sick or dying.
> 
> *Wants are non-essential items for life. They are individualistic desires that change overtime, and may lead to mental distress if they are not met (mental distress including disappointment and annoyance).* For example, if a person is not able to receive the vinyl that they want to buy, they may feel upset or have a negative impact on their emotional wellbeing, but will not lead to adverse outcomes if not met.
## Specific Needs
Every individual has the *same* six basic needs. These include:
- **S**afety and security –> having a safe and secure environment in a physical, emotional, and financial context
- **H**ealth –> having the minimum bodily function to work in society, such as being physically active and having good nutrition
- **E**ducation –> having the formal training in the knowledge and skills necessary to succeed in life
- **S**ense of identity –> self=understanding, feeling confident in their purpose in life
- **E**mployment –> earning an income, which allows for independence, thus satisfying needs and wants
- **A**dequate standard of living –> primary needs (such as *food* for growth and development, *clothing* for extreme weather protection, and *shelter* for protection from danger), sustainable environments (*clean air*, *surroundings*, *water*), and lifestyle (adequate rest)

> [!Example] Different Need Prioritisation for Different Individuals
> However, despite every individual having the same six needs, the prioritisation of these needs are *dependent on their own individual circumstances*. 
> - A young person may need to prioritise their sense of identity and education to help improve their wellbeing.
> - Homeless people might need to prioritise their safety and security, adequate standard of living, and health, in order to have better wellbeing.

## Maslow’s Hierarchy

> [!Tip] Maslow’s Hierarchy
> Developed in 1943, Abraham Maslow developed a theory that described and explained human motivation through five levels on a pyramid. This is an outdated theory that described individual’s specific needs.
> 
> However, Maslow’s hierarchy was critiqued for lacking scientific evidence, having a rigid structure (suggesting that needs MUST be met in a specific order), having cultural bias, being one-dimensional, and over-simplifying human motivation.
## Satisfaction of Needs and Wants

> [!Tip] Satisfaction of Needs and Wants
> To satisfy needs and wants, people can set goals to provide an incentive to perform well and build self-esteem. These goals can be set through the SMART framework.
> - **S**pecific
> - **M**easurable
> - **A**chievable
> - **R**elevant
> - **T**ime-bound
>   
>  > [!Example] 
>  > For example, a person can set a goal that follows this framework to be healthier over the next 8 weeks.
>  > 
>  > > Maintain a consistent gym routine **by doing 2 sets of arm exercises** \[*relevant, specific, and measurable*] for **three days** \[*measurable*] over **eight weeks** \[*time-bound*].
> 
> > [!Example]
> > Another example could be the following:
> > > Get a **Band 5-6** \[*relevant & specific*] in all subjects by **regularly studying** \[*specific*] **every day** \[*measurable*] over the **next two years** \[*time-bound*].

# Resources
> [!Note] Students Learn To
> ▪ describe a range of resources and explain how they assist in satisfying specific needs
> 
> ▪ propose how resources could be interchanged to enhance wellbeing in a variety of situations
> 
> ▪ outline strategies individuals use to conserve human and non-human resources
## Defining Resources

> [!Abstract] Definition
> Resources are things people used to achieve [[#^c9848a|goals]] (thus, to satisfy specific needs and wants), whether it be an object, person, idea, concept, skill, or quality that may assist in achieving these goals.

## Specific Resources
Resources are split into **human** and **non-human**, **formal** and **informal**, and **economic** and **non-economic** resources. These types of resources can contribute to the [[#Specific Needs|specific needs]] of a person.

> [!Tip]
> Human and non-human resources are the most important categories to remember.

### Human and Non-Human
Human resources are intangible resources, such as skills, abilities, talents, knowledge, initiative, and etc. Non-human resources are tangible objects, such as computers, food, vehicles, and etc.

> [!Example] Relating to [[#Specific Needs]]
> A person’s creativity may contribute to their sense of identity. Similarly, food may help contribute to a person’s adequate standard of living.

### Formal and Informal
Formal resources are resources organised by the community, usually with a formal leadership system and set hours of operation. Informal resources are vice versa, and do not require a formal leadership system or set hours of operation, and thus, are available informally.

> [!Example] Relating to [[#Specific Needs]]
> Pre-schools and libraries are formal resources, fulfilling needs such as education. Family and friends are informal resources, allowing for the fulfilment of needs such as safety and security.
### Economic and Non-Economic
Economic resources are resources used to achieve economic gain or complete economic goals. Non-economic resources are vice versa, and are non-financial resources.

> [!Example] Relating to [[#Specific Needs]]
> Income (welfare) and property wealth are economic resources, helping with needs such as safety and security. Communication skills and energy are non-economic resources, assisting with needs such as employment and education.
### Other

> [!Tip]
> Resources can also be looked through the perspective of the following categories:
> - Personal (human)
> - Interpersonal (human)
> - Money (non-human)
> - Material possessions (non-human)
> - Natural environment (non-human)
> - Global (human & non-human)
> - Community (human & non-human)
## Interchangeability of Resources
Resources can be exchanged or interchanged, such as a time-poor worker exchanging their money (a non-human resource) for a house cleaner, which requires skills (a human resource).

Most scenarios of resource interchangeability will enhance wellbeing through the development of a people’s sense of identity and social connections. It will also allow for the accessibility of other resources, which will also enhance wellbeing.

> [!Example] Example Scenarios
> - Doing chores around the house for money: skills and time (**human**) –> money (**non-human**)
> 	  - Enhances *individual* wellbeing (money for savings)
> 	  - Enhances *group* wellbeing (parents don’t need to spend too much time)
> - Driving lessons from parents in exchange for babysitting: knowledge and time (**human**) –> knowledge (**human**)
> 	- Enhances *individual* wellbeing (learning new skills, enhancing sense of identity)
> 	- Enhances *group* wellbeing (saved time and service from parents)
> - University degree in part-time as a sole parent: time (**human**) and money (**non-human**) –> education (**human**)
> 	- Enhances *individual* wellbeing (learning new skills, fulfilling needs such as education and sense of identity, as well as supporting economic and emotional wellbeing)
> 	- Enhances *group* wellbeing (more opportunity for employment to support family, fulfilling needs such as employment)
## Resource Sustainability
In regards to the field of resource sustainability, there are basic concepts that must be established:
- Resources must be sustained or conserved.
- Individuals are the consumers of resources.
- Resources can be split into two more categories, *renewable* and *non-renewable*.
### Strategies to Sustain Resources

| Resources (*Human* and **Non-Human**) | Strategies                                                   |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| *Intelligence*                        | Use technology, read and write, go to school                 |
| **Money**                             | Budget and invest                                            |
| *First aid skills*                    | Join courses, review, practice                               |
| **Water**                             | Repurposing water, timed usage, and use sustainability taps  |
| *Cooking*                             | Receive knowledge from parents and culture, practice cooking |
| **Roads**                             | Council repairs, restrictions, and roadwork                  |
