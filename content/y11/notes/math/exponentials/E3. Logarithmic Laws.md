# Logarithmic Laws

> [!Abstract] Overview
> $$
> log_a x + log_a y = log_a (x y) \
> log_a x - log_a y = log_a (x/y) \
> log_a (x^n) = n log_a x \
> \ 
> log_a (1/x) \
> = log_a (x^(-1)) = -log_a x \
> \
> log_a x = (log_b x)/(log_b a) \
> $$
## Logarithm of a Product and Quotient
When dealing with two logarithms added to each other, it is called the logarithm of a product.
$$
log_a x + log_a y = log_a (x y)
$$

> [!Example]
> $$
> log_7 10 = log_7 2 + log_7 5 \
> \
> log_4 2 + log_4 8 = log_4 (2 times 8) \
> = log_4 (16) \
> therefore = 2
> $$

Similarly, when dealing with the difference of two logarithms, it is called the logarithm of a quotient.
$$
log_a x - log_a y = log_a (x/y)
$$
> [!Example]
> $$
> log_7 0.4 = log_7 2 - log_7 5 \
> = log_7 (2/5)
> \
> log_5 50 - log_5 2 = log_5 (50/2) \
> = log_5 (25) \
> therefore = 2
> $$
## Logarithm of a Power
When the logarithm is dealing with a number raised to a power of $n$, it is called the logarithm of a power.
$$
log_a (x^n)= n log_a x
$$
### Bonus: Logarithm of a Power with Negative Powers
When dealing with negative indices, you can use the following forms.
$$
log_a (1/x) = -log_a x \
log_a (x^(-1)) = -log_a x
$$
## Change of Base Law

> [!Tip]
> This is especially handy when you have a calculator that is not capable of using custom bases for logarithms. Although some NESA-approved calculators (e.g. CASIO fx-8200au) are capable of this function, it is still important to know the law in case that it is asked of you in an examination.

When dealing with a logarithm that does not have a base of 10 or if one wants to change the base of a logarithm, you can use the change of base law.

$$
log_a x = (log_b x)/(log_b a)
$$
This is where $a$ is the base of the new logarithm, and $b$ is the base of the old logarithm.
